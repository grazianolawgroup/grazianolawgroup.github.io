Modifying Child Custody and Support Orders in Florida

Life doesn't stand still after a divorce or paternity case is finalized — but Florida courts won't reopen a custody or support order just because circumstances shifted a little. Here's what it actually takes.

Why Modification Isn't Automatic

Once a Florida court enters a final judgment establishing a parenting plan, time-sharing schedule, or child support order, that judgment is meant to provide stability for the child and both parents. Courts don't revisit these orders every time something changes. Instead, the law requires the party asking for a change to prove that the change meets a specific legal threshold before a judge will even consider modifying the terms. Understanding that threshold, and what it does and doesn't cover, is the difference between a modification petition that succeeds and one that gets dismissed.

Modifying Time-Sharing and Parental Responsibility

Under Fla. Stat. § 61.13(3), a parenting plan, time-sharing schedule, or parental responsibility determination cannot be modified without a showing of a substantial and material change in circumstances, combined with a finding that the modification serves the child's best interests. This is a two-part test: the change alone isn't enough if modifying the plan wouldn't actually benefit the child, and a clear benefit to the child isn't enough if there wasn't a substantial change to begin with.

Florida law does carve out one specific scenario: if the parents lived more than 50 miles apart when the last time-sharing order was entered and one parent later moves within 50 miles of the other, that move can itself qualify as a substantial and material change, opening the door to a modification request. Outside of that rule, courts weigh the same best-interest factors used to create the original parenting plan — each parent's demonstrated involvement, the child's stability, any evidence of domestic violence or substance abuse, and more — when deciding whether and how to adjust time-sharing.

A pattern of one parent refusing to honor the time-sharing schedule without proper cause can also support a modification, on top of remedies like makeup time-sharing, fee awards, or contempt.

Modifying Child Support

Florida courts retain continuing jurisdiction over child support orders. Under Fla. Stat. § 61.13(1)(a)2, a court can modify the amount and terms of support when it's in the child's best interests, when there's been a substantial change in the circumstances of the parties, or when events like the child turning 18, graduating high school, marrying, joining the military, or dying change what's owed.

In practice, the child support guidelines themselves supply a useful benchmark. Under Fla. Stat. § 61.30(1)(b), if recalculating support under the current guidelines schedule would produce a monthly amount that differs from the existing order by at least 15% or $50, whichever is greater, that gap is treated as evidence of a substantial change in circumstances sufficient to support a modification. (In Title IV-D cases handled through the Department of Revenue, a smaller 10%-or-$25 gap can trigger an administrative review without a separate court showing.) A significant, ongoing change in either parent's income, a new child care or health insurance cost, or a real shift in the time-sharing split can all move the guidelines number enough to clear that bar.

What Usually Doesn't Count

Not every life change moves the needle. Courts are generally skeptical of changes that are temporary, self-inflicted, or that the parties could have anticipated when the original order was entered. A parent who voluntarily quits a job, takes a lower-paying position, or works fewer hours to reduce a support obligation typically won't get relief — the court can impute income based on the parent's earning capacity instead of lowering support to match the reduced paycheck. Ordinary disagreements about parenting style, a parent's preference for more time, or a temporary rough patch in the co-parenting relationship usually fall short of "substantial and material" as well. The stronger cases involve concrete, lasting, and largely involuntary changes: a real job loss, a significant and sustained income change, a move, a documented safety concern, or a meaningful shift in the child's own needs.

The Process for Seeking a Modification

A modification isn't a simple form filed with the clerk. It starts with a supplemental petition filed in the circuit court that has continuing jurisdiction over the case — usually the county where the original judgment was entered or where the child and a parent currently reside. The other parent must be served and given the chance to respond, and most circuits require mediation before a modification case can be set for trial. Support modifications require updated financial affidavits and documentation of the income or expense changes driving the request. Custody and time-sharing modifications typically involve more fact-intensive evidence, sometimes including a guardian ad litem, social investigation, or expert testimony, depending on what's at stake.

Support and Time-Sharing Are Legally Separate

It's tempting to treat child support and time-sharing as leverage against each other, but Florida law doesn't allow it. A parent who isn't receiving court-ordered support still must honor the other parent's time-sharing rights, and a parent who is denied time-sharing must still pay support as ordered. Withholding one to punish noncompliance with the other is a common and costly mistake — it can expose the withholding parent to contempt, fee awards, or a modification working against them, on top of not actually fixing the underlying problem. The right response to either kind of noncompliance is to go back to court, not to take matters into your own hands.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as a substantial change in circumstances for custody modification?

Florida courts look for a change that is significant, unanticipated, and not something the parties could have foreseen when the original parenting plan was entered, such as a parent's relocation, a documented pattern of denying time-sharing, a substance abuse or mental health issue affecting the child's safety, or a major shift in the child's needs. Ordinary friction between co-parents or a parent's preference for a different schedule usually isn't enough.

Can I modify child support without going back to court?

Generally no. Absent a Department of Revenue administrative review in a Title IV-D case, a change to a child support order requires filing a supplemental petition for modification and obtaining a new court order. Simply agreeing between the parents to pay a different amount does not modify the legal obligation on record.

Does losing my job automatically reduce my child support?

No. A job loss can support a modification petition, but the reduction isn't automatic and isn't retroactive to the date you stopped working, it applies from the date the petition is filed absent unusual circumstances. If the unemployment is voluntary or the court finds you're underemployed, the court can impute income instead of lowering support.

Can I stop paying child support if my ex won't follow the time-sharing schedule?

No. Florida law treats child support and time-sharing as separate obligations. A parent who is denied time-sharing must continue paying support and ask the court to enforce or modify time-sharing; a parent who isn't paid support must still honor the time-sharing schedule. Withholding either as self-help can backfire in court.

Do I need a lawyer to modify a custody or support order?

It isn't required, but modification petitions require proving a substantial and material change in circumstances with evidence and, for support, updated financial affidavits and documentation. An attorney can help identify whether the facts meet the legal standard before you file, which affects both the odds of success and the cost of getting there.
