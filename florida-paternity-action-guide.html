Establishing Paternity in Florida: What the Process Involves

Signing a hospital acknowledgment form feels like a formality — but in Florida, it's one of several legal paths to establishing paternity, each with its own timeline and consequences.

Why Establishing Paternity Matters

When a child is born to married parents, Florida law presumes the mother's husband is the legal father. When parents aren't married, that presumption doesn't apply, and paternity has to be established through one of the methods set out in Chapter 742 of the Florida Statutes before either parent has enforceable rights or obligations tied to legal fatherhood — things like child support, time-sharing, inheritance rights, and the ability to be listed on the birth certificate.

Three Paths to Establishing Paternity

Florida recognizes a few different routes to a legal determination of paternity. The most common is a voluntary acknowledgment of paternity, often signed at the hospital shortly after birth, or later through a notarized form or one witnessed by two people and signed under penalty of perjury. A second path runs through the Department of Revenue, which can administratively establish paternity as part of its child support enforcement role. The third is a circuit court action under Fla. Stat. § 742.011, which can be brought by the mother, a man who believes he's the father, or the child, whenever paternity hasn't already been established some other way.

The 60-Day Window to Rescind a Voluntary Acknowledgment

A properly executed voluntary acknowledgment creates a rebuttable presumption of paternity, but either signer has 60 days from the date it was signed, or until a related administrative or court proceeding involving the child, whichever comes first, to rescind it. Once that window closes, the acknowledgment stands as an establishment of paternity, and it generally can only be challenged afterward on narrow grounds: fraud, duress, or a material mistake of fact, with the person challenging it bearing the burden of proof. Child support obligations tied to the acknowledgment normally continue during any such challenge unless a court finds good cause to suspend them.

What a Paternity Case Actually Decides

It's easy to assume that once paternity is on record, everything else — time-sharing, decision-making, child support — follows automatically. It doesn't. Florida law treats the paternity determination and the parenting and support arrangements as separate steps. Even after paternity is established by acknowledgment or administrative order, a parent typically still needs to bring an action under Fla. Stat. § 742.011 to put a parenting plan, time-sharing schedule, parental responsibility arrangement, and child support order in place. In practice, many paternity cases handle both steps together, but the legal establishment of who the father is and the court's decisions about the child's day-to-day arrangements are not the same thing.

Genetic Testing When Paternity Is Disputed

When there's a real dispute about biological fatherhood, either party can ask the court to order scientific testing. If a party willfully refuses to submit to testing without good cause, the court can decide the paternity question against that party. Where testing does happen, the results are typically the most persuasive evidence in the case, and a high probability of paternity from a properly conducted test is difficult to overcome.

Disestablishing Paternity Later

Florida also has a process for a man to disestablish paternity or end a child support obligation if he later learns he isn't the biological father, under Fla. Stat. § 742.18. He has to file a petition supported by newly discovered evidence and recent genetic testing showing he isn't the father, and he must be current on support or have a just-cause explanation for any arrears. Even then, relief isn't automatic: the court will deny the petition if the man took certain actions after learning he might not be the biological father, such as marrying the mother while known as the reputed father, formally acknowledging paternity in a sworn statement, or ignoring a court or agency order to submit to testing. Relief, when granted, only affects prospective support and parental rights; it doesn't create a right to recover support already paid.

Getting It Right From the Start

Because a voluntary acknowledgment becomes very difficult to undo after 60 days, and because paternity, time-sharing, and support are handled through different legal mechanisms, it's worth understanding exactly what you're signing and what still needs to happen afterward — whether you're the one establishing paternity or the one being asked to sign.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How is paternity established in Florida?

Florida recognizes several paths under Chapter 742: a voluntary acknowledgment of paternity signed by both parents, an administrative order from the Department of Revenue, or a circuit court judgment in a paternity action under Fla. Stat. 742.011. Any of these establishes legal paternity.

What is the 60-day window to rescind a voluntary acknowledgment?

Either parent who signs a notarized or properly witnessed voluntary acknowledgment of paternity can rescind it within 60 days of signing, or by the date of a related administrative or judicial proceeding, whichever comes first. After that window closes, the acknowledgment can generally only be challenged in court based on fraud, duress, or a material mistake of fact.

Does establishing paternity automatically set child support and time-sharing?

No. Under Florida law, establishing paternity and setting parental responsibility, time-sharing, and child support are handled separately. Even after paternity is established through an acknowledgment or administrative order, a parent generally still needs to bring an action under Fla. Stat. 742.011 to get a parenting plan, time-sharing schedule, and child support order in place.

Can a father later disestablish paternity in Florida?

In limited circumstances. Fla. Stat. 742.18 allows a man to petition to disestablish paternity or end a child support obligation if he presents newly discovered evidence and genetic testing showing he is not the biological father, among other requirements. But the statute blocks this relief if he took certain actions after learning he might not be the father, such as marrying the mother while known as the reputed father or ignoring a court order to submit to testing.

What happens if a man disputes paternity?

Either party can ask the court to order scientific (genetic) testing. If a party willfully refuses to submit to testing without good cause, the court can rule against that party on the paternity question. Test results showing a high probability of paternity are strong evidence in establishing or confirming legal fatherhood.
