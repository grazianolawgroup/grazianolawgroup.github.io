Postnuptial Agreements in Florida vs. a Prenup

Missing the window to sign a prenup before the wedding doesn't close the door on a marital agreement — but a postnup signed after the vows faces a tougher legal standard than one signed before.

What a Postnuptial Agreement Is

A postnuptial agreement, or postnup, is a contract between spouses who are already married, addressing how property, debt, and support would be divided if the marriage later ends in divorce or death. Functionally, it covers much of the same ground as a prenup — the difference is timing, and timing changes the legal analysis considerably.

No Statute, Different Standard

Unlike prenups, which are governed by Florida's Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (Fla. Stat. § 61.079), postnups are not covered by that statute. Instead, Florida courts evaluate postnups under general contract law principles and the framework set out by the Florida Supreme Court in Casto v. Casto, 508 So. 2d 330 (Fla. 1987). Under Casto, a postnup is enforceable where each spouse made a full and fair financial disclosure before signing, the agreement was entered into freely and voluntarily, without fraud, duress, coercion, or overreaching, and the terms weren't unconscionable given the circumstances at the time.

Why Full Financial Disclosure Actually Matters Here

This is the biggest practical difference from a prenup. A prenup can, in some circumstances, be enforced even without full disclosure if the challenging spouse already had a general understanding of the marital finances, or disclosure was validly waived. A postnup gets closer scrutiny: full, fair disclosure of the marital estate, or the challenging spouse's own independent, reasonably detailed knowledge of it, is central to whether the agreement holds up. The disclosure doesn't need to be exact to the dollar, but it needs to give the other spouse a genuine picture of what's being divided or waived.

Why Courts Look Harder at Postnups

Once a couple is married, the law recognizes that spouses already owe each other duties of fairness and good faith that engaged couples don't yet have. That existing relationship creates more room for one spouse to pressure the other, whether through financial leverage, emotional dynamics, or simply the practical difficulty of walking away from an existing marriage. Because of that dynamic, a spouse challenging a postnup can have it set aside by showing it was the product of fraud, duress, coercion, or overreaching, or that it makes an unreasonable provision for that spouse — which shifts the burden to the other spouse to prove adequate disclosure or independent knowledge.

Common Reasons Couples Sign a Postnup

Postnups tend to come up in response to something specific that happens during a marriage rather than as a routine planning step. Frequent scenarios include one spouse starting or selling a business, a significant inheritance or gift one spouse wants to keep separate, a period of marital difficulty — including infidelity — that the couple wants to address with financial terms rather than divorce, one spouse taking on significant new debt, or a couple who simply never got around to a prenup before the wedding and wants similar protections in place now.

What a Postnup Cannot Do

Like a prenup, a postnup cannot predetermine child support or time-sharing arrangements; those remain subject to the court's independent review of the child's best interests at the time they're decided. And a postnup that's fundamentally unfair to one spouse, given the full circumstances at signing, risks being thrown out even with disclosure in place.

Getting It Right the First Time

Because postnups face closer scrutiny than prenups, the details matter even more: complete and documented financial disclosure, each spouse represented by their own attorney, and enough time to review and negotiate the terms without pressure. An agreement drafted carefully at the outset is far more likely to hold up years later if it's ever tested.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a postnuptial agreement?

A postnuptial agreement, or postnup, is a contract signed by spouses after they're already married that addresses how property, debt, and support would be handled if the marriage later ends in divorce or death. It serves the same basic purpose as a prenup, just entered into after the wedding.

Are postnups legal in Florida?

Yes. Florida courts recognize and enforce postnuptial agreements, though they are not governed by the same statute as prenups. Instead, their enforceability is evaluated under general contract law principles and the standard set out in Casto v. Casto.

Does a postnup require full financial disclosure in Florida?

Yes, and this is one of the biggest differences from a prenup. Under the Casto standard, each spouse must make a full and fair disclosure of their finances, or the challenging spouse must already have had a general and reasonable understanding of the marital estate, before the postnup can be enforced.

Why do courts scrutinize postnups more than prenups?

Because spouses already owe each other fiduciary-like duties of fairness once married, courts apply closer scrutiny to postnups to guard against one spouse using the existing relationship to pressure the other into an unfair agreement.

Why would a couple sign a postnup instead of divorcing?

Couples often use postnups to address a specific issue that has come up during the marriage, such as one spouse starting a business, an inheritance, past infidelity, or uneven financial contributions, without ending the marriage. It can also be used simply because the couple didn't sign a prenup before the wedding.
