Florida Durable Power of Attorney: A Practical Guide

A will protects your family after you're gone. A durable power of attorney protects you while you're still here but unable to act for yourself.

What a Durable Power of Attorney Actually Does

A power of attorney (POA) is a document in which you (the "principal") authorize someone else (your "agent" or "attorney-in-fact") to act on your behalf — signing documents, managing accounts, paying bills, handling property, or making other financial and legal decisions for you. In Florida, a POA is durable by default under current law, meaning it remains effective even if you later become incapacitated, unless the document specifically says otherwise. That durability is exactly why the document matters: it's designed for the moment you can no longer manage things yourself.

What It Can — and Can't — Cover

Florida law allows a POA to grant broad authority: banking and financial transactions, real estate transactions, business operations, insurance and retirement benefit decisions, tax matters, and more. Florida is also fairly strict about certain high-risk powers — actions like making gifts of your property, changing beneficiary designations, or creating or amending a trust generally must be spelled out specifically in the document itself. A generic, boilerplate POA often won't include this specific language, which can leave your agent unable to act when it matters most.

Choosing Your Agent

This is the most important decision in the document. Your agent will potentially have significant control over your finances, so the choice should come down to trust and judgment — not simply whoever is closest geographically or oldest in the family. Consider:

  • Does this person handle their own finances responsibly?
  • Will they act in your interest even under family pressure?
  • Are they willing and able to take on the responsibility?
  • Should you name a successor agent in case your first choice can't serve?

You can also name co-agents, though requiring them to act jointly can create practical friction — most people either name one agent with successors, or co-agents who can each act independently.

Why "Durable" Matters More Than People Realize

Without a durable power of attorney in place, if you become incapacitated — through illness, injury, or age — your loved ones may have no legal authority to manage your affairs. The alternative is a court-supervised guardianship proceeding, which is slower, more expensive, more public, and puts the decision about who manages your affairs in a judge's hands rather than yours. A properly drafted durable POA is, in a very real sense, the document that keeps that decision in your control.

When to Put One in Place

The honest answer: before you need it. A POA only works if you're mentally competent when you sign it — you can't create one after a diagnosis has already progressed to the point of incapacity. Most estate planning attorneys recommend executing a durable POA at the same time as a will, regardless of age, and revisiting it after major life events such as marriage, divorce, or a move to a new state.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Does my agent need to be a family member?

No. Florida law simply requires your agent be a competent adult (or a qualified financial institution for certain matters). Many people choose a spouse, adult child, or sibling, but a trusted friend or professional fiduciary can serve as well.

Can I revoke a power of attorney once it's signed?

Yes, as long as you're competent to do so. Revocation should be done in writing and communicated clearly to your agent and to any institutions relying on the document, such as banks.

Is a power of attorney the same as a healthcare surrogate designation?

No — they cover different things. A durable POA generally addresses financial and legal matters. Medical decision-making is handled separately through a healthcare surrogate designation and living will, though many people execute both as part of the same planning session.

What happens if I become incapacitated without a POA in place?

A loved one would typically need to petition the court for guardianship in order to gain legal authority over your affairs — a process that takes time, costs more, and is subject to court oversight and public record.
