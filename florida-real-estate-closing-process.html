The Florida Real Estate Closing Process, Step by Step

A signed contract feels like the finish line. In reality, it's the starting point of a process with several moving parts that all have to line up by closing day.

Contract to Closing: The Big Picture

Once a purchase and sale agreement is signed, a Florida real estate transaction generally moves through several parallel tracks at once: title work, financing (if applicable), inspections, and closing preparation. Each track has its own timeline, and the closing date on the contract only works if all of them finish on schedule.

Title Search and Title Insurance

Early in the process, a title company or real estate attorney orders a title search on the property — reviewing public records for liens, judgments, easements, boundary issues, or ownership gaps that could affect the buyer's ability to take clear title. Based on that search, a title insurance policy is issued to protect against covered title defects, including ones that didn't turn up in the search itself. This step is foundational; problems caught here are far easier to resolve before closing than after.

Inspections and Due Diligence

Buyers typically have a due diligence or inspection period defined in the contract to have the property inspected and to negotiate repairs or credits based on what's found. This period has a hard deadline — missing it can mean losing the right to negotiate or, depending on the contract, losing your right to cancel with your deposit protected.

The Role of Escrow

Earnest money deposits, and ultimately the closing funds themselves, are held by an escrow agent (often the title company or a real estate attorney) until closing. This protects both parties — the buyer's deposit isn't released to the seller prematurely, and the seller has assurance the funds are secured before transferring the deed.

Closing Disclosure and Final Walkthrough

Shortly before closing, buyers using financing receive a Closing Disclosure detailing final loan terms and closing costs, which has to be reviewed within a required waiting period before closing can occur. Around the same time, buyers typically do a final walkthrough of the property to confirm its condition hasn't changed and that any agreed repairs were completed.

Closing Day: What Actually Happens

At closing, the buyer (and lender, if financing) provide funds, the seller signs the deed transferring ownership, and both sides sign a stack of closing documents. Once funds are disbursed and the deed is recorded with the county, ownership officially transfers. In Florida, closings are commonly handled by a title company or real estate attorney rather than in a courtroom or bank branch.

Common Delays and How to Avoid Them

The most frequent causes of closing delays are title issues discovered late, financing conditions that take longer to clear than expected, and repair negotiations that drag past the inspection deadline. Building in realistic timelines, responding promptly to requests from your lender or title company, and having a real estate attorney review the contract before you sign are the most effective ways to keep a transaction on schedule.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a typical Florida closing take from contract to close?

It varies widely based on financing, title complexity, and negotiated timelines, but 30 to 45 days is common for financed transactions. Cash transactions can close considerably faster.

Do I need an attorney if I'm already using a title company?

A title company handles the closing mechanics, but it doesn't represent either party's legal interests. A real estate attorney can review the contract, address title issues, and advocate for you specifically — something a neutral title company isn't positioned to do.

What happens if the closing date in the contract can't be met?

Most Florida contracts include provisions for extending the closing date or specify what happens if a party isn't ready to close, though the specifics depend heavily on the exact contract language, which is one reason a careful initial contract review matters.

Can I back out of a contract after signing?

It depends on the contract terms and where you are in the process. Contingencies like financing and inspection periods generally provide the clearest ability to cancel; once those periods pass, backing out becomes more legally and financially consequential.
