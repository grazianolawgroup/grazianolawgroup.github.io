Florida School Bus & School Zone Camera Tickets: What Drivers Need to Know

School bus stop-arm cameras are now active across Broward and Miami-Dade, and school zone speed cameras are running — or being reconsidered — in communities throughout Miami-Dade, Broward, and Palm Beach. They're not one program, and they're not equally settled law.

Two Different Camera Programs, Two Different Rules

It's easy to lump "school camera tickets" together, but Florida actually runs two distinct programs with different statutes, different technology, and â as of this year â very different legal footing across South Florida. One targets drivers who pass a stopped school bus with its stop arm extended. The other targets speeding through an active school zone. Knowing which one generated your notice matters, and so does knowing which county issued it, because enforcement history and legal status vary from one South Florida county to the next.

School Bus Stop-Arm Cameras: Active in Broward and Miami-Dade

Broward County Public Schools equipped roughly 1,300 buses with stop-arm camera technology ahead of the 2026–27 school year, following a 30-day warning period before real citations began. Miami-Dade runs a similar program on close to 900 buses through the same camera vendor, BusPatrol â though its path there was rockier: the original rollout was suspended after reporting found drivers were sometimes fined for passing legally on the far side of a raised median, and it only resumed full enforcement in May 2026 with added oversight. Under Florida law, a violation in either county carries a $225 civil penalty and does not add points to your license. Citations are reviewed before they're mailed out, but that review step hasn't always caught every error â camera angle, timing, signage, and whether the vehicle in the video is actually yours are all worth checking before assuming a notice is accurate. Drivers who disagree can request a hearing through Florida's Division of Administrative Hearings, generally within 30 days of the notice.

School Zone Speed Cameras: A Program Still Being Tested in Court

Separately, Florida law authorizes municipalities to use automated speed detection systems in school zones during school hours, issuing a $100 civil penalty to a vehicle's registered owner for driving more than 10 mph over the posted limit. Like the bus program, these tickets don't carry points and generally aren't reported to insurers. Programs under this statute have been active in various South Florida municipalities, including parts of Palm Beach County.

What's different is that this program has run into real legal trouble, starting in Broward. In April 2026, a Broward County traffic hearing officer dismissed a batch of pending school zone speed camera cases after ruling that the detection device generating the citations wasn't an approved enforcement tool under Florida law. That ruling had ripple effects: Palm Beach County's commission cited it directly in May 2026 when it declined to move forward with a planned speed-camera ordinance for unincorporated school zones, though the county left the door open to revisit the program later. That doesn't mean every school zone camera ticket is invalid, or that it applies the same way in every municipality â it depends on the specific device, vendor, and jurisdiction involved, and the legal landscape here is actively shifting. But it does mean a school zone speeding notice, wherever in South Florida it was issued, is worth a second look rather than an automatic payment.

Should You Pay It or Have It Reviewed?

For a bus stop-arm citation, the main questions are usually factual: was it clearly your vehicle, was the stop arm actually extended and visible, and does the timeline in the notice match what you remember â Miami-Dade's own troubled history with median-related errors is a reminder that "the camera caught it" isn't the same as "the citation is correct." For a school zone speed citation, there's an added legal question on top of the factual ones â whether the specific camera system used is even a valid enforcement tool where you were cited. Given that uncertainty, paying immediately isn't always the fastest or cheapest path; having the notice reviewed first often is.

What to Do When a Notice Arrives

Note the response deadline printed on the notice first â these programs have their own timelines, separate from a standard traffic citation, and missing one can limit your options. From there, gather what you can: the date and time on the notice, any photo or video evidence provided, and who was actually driving. Whether the right move is to pay, request a hearing, or challenge the underlying enforcement device is a case-by-case call that can depend on which county issued the notice, and it's one worth making with accurate information rather than guessing.

Common Questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Do school bus stop-arm or school zone speed camera tickets carry points?

No. Both programs are structured as civil penalties rather than moving violations, so a Notice of Violation from either program generally does not add points to your license, whether it was issued in Miami-Dade, Broward, or Palm Beach County.

Is it true some Florida school zone speed camera tickets have been thrown out?

Yes, in some jurisdictions. A Broward County traffic hearing officer dismissed a batch of school zone speed camera cases after finding the specific detection device used had not been approved under Florida law. That ruling was significant enough that Palm Beach County's commission cited it when pausing its own planned school-zone camera program in unincorporated areas. None of this means every citation will be dismissed — outcomes depend on the device, vendor, and jurisdiction involved, and the law in this area is actively being litigated.

Has Miami-Dade's school bus camera program had problems before?

Yes. Miami-Dade's original stop-arm camera program was suspended after reporting found some drivers were being fined for passing legally on the far side of a raised median. It relaunched with stronger oversight, but that history is a good reason to have any citation reviewed rather than assume it's accurate.

Should I just pay a school bus or school zone camera ticket to make it go away?

Not automatically. Given the ongoing legal questions around device approval for school zone speed cameras, and given that at least one South Florida camera program has a documented history of issuing incorrect citations, it's worth having the notice looked at before assuming it's valid.
